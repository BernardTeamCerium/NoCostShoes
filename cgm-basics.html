CGM Basics: What a Continuous Glucose Monitor Does

October 29, 2026 · 5 min read — A small sensor, readings around the clock, and far fewer fingersticks — here’s how CGMs work.

For decades, checking your glucose meant pricking a finger — a snapshot of a single moment, several times a day. A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) replaces those snapshots with a around-the-clock picture: a small sensor worn on the body measures glucose every few minutes and sends readings straight to your smartphone or a receiver.

How a CGM Works

A CGM sensor — about the size of a few stacked coins — is applied to the back of the upper arm or abdomen. A tiny filament under the skin measures glucose in the fluid between cells. The sensor transmits readings automatically, builds trend graphs, and can alert you before you go too high or too low. Most sensors are worn for 10 to 15 days and are replaced at home in seconds.

The Systems We Carry

  • FreeStyle Libre 3 Plus (Abbott) — a small, discreet sensor that streams readings to a compatible smartphone every minute, with customizable alarms.
  • Dexcom G6 & G7 — real-time readings with sharing features that keep family and caregivers in the loop.
  • Dexcom Stelo — the first over-the-counter CGM in the U.S., designed for adults with type 2 diabetes who aren’t on insulin.

Is a CGM Covered?

CGMs are widely covered by Medicare for patients managing diabetes with insulin, and by many commercial plans — patients must meet eligibility coverage criteria, and a prescription is required. The practical difference is significant: trend data helps you and your doctor see how meals, activity, and medication actually affect your glucose, rather than guessing between fingersticks.
